What does "chance your project worked" actually mean?

It is the probability that your change genuinely moved the process, given how your chart looks, starting from a neutral 50:50 chance that it works. The starting chance is fixed on purpose, so it cannot be talked up. It combines how often a chart this good appears by luck when nothing has changed with how often a genuine change would produce one. It is a probability, not a verdict, and it rests on the assumptions below.

Why judge it against a "smallest improvement that matters"?

Declaring success on any shift at all rewards flukes: tiny, worthless improvements can look impressive in small samples. Deciding the bar in advance — the gain that would actually justify keeping the change — and then asking "did we clear it?" keeps you honest. The tool gives two graded answers: the chance that anything real happened at all, and the chance that what happened reaches your bar. A project can be genuinely improved and still fall short of the bar; that is a real finding, not a failure of the maths.

How is this different from a run chart or the earlier calculator?

Run chart rules need long, well-behaved series and fire on noise in short, busy-department data. The earlier calculator assumed the true improvement was exactly the one you observed, which flatters small samples. This tool instead allows the true improvement to be anywhere from nothing up to a generous ceiling, lets your data say where it probably sits, and is deliberately cautious: with little data it refuses to hand out high confidence, whichever way the result would flatter you.

What this tool cannot see

Surges, staff sickness, case-mix shifts, season and other things that changed at the same time as your change are invisible to it. If any of them lined up with your intervention, part of the shift may belong to them rather than to you. It also assumes consecutive points are independent; in a real department, nearby points often drift together, which makes the percentage flattering. Treat the result as one piece of evidence, alongside everything else you know about the project.

Assumptions behind the estimate

Each data point is the median of the same number of patients, drawn from a lognormal distribution (times) or a binomial (proportions), with the same spread in every phase. The baseline is steady, with no trend, and the change took effect immediately and stayed. Two possibilities are compared, each starting at 50:50: no change, or a genuine improvement whose true size is assumed, before looking at your end data, to be equally likely anywhere between just above zero and five times your stated bar. Your chart's medians are simulated thousands of times under each possibility, so figures can wobble by about a percentage point. The variation setting is an assumption unless it was estimated from your baseline data. The assessment compares your final cycle with your baseline; with more than one cycle, the table shows what each stage added.

For QI teams: the honest question at the end of a project is not "did the chart move?" but "did it move more than luck, and more than the least we would accept?"
